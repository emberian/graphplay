THE STRENGTH OF WEAK COUPLING

arXiv:2512.08141v1 [quant-ph] 9 Dec 2025

ALASTAIR KAY AND CHRISTINO TAMON

Abstract. A paradoxical idea in quantum transport is that attaching weakly-coupled edges to a
large base graph creates high-fidelity quantum state transfer. We provide a mathematical treatment
that rigorously prove this folklore idea. Our proofs are elementary and build upon the FeshbachSchur method from perturbation theory. We also show the idea is effective in circumventing Anderson localization in spin chains and finding speedups in hitting times useful for quantum search.

1. Introduction
A continuous-time quantum walk on a graph G is a time-varying unitary matrix U (t) = exp(−itA)
where A is the adjacency matrix of G. This framework is useful for developing quantum algorithms (see [16, 17]), which had culminated in a quantum walk algorithm with provable exponential
speedup [9] (a guarantee missing in both Shor factoring and Grover search).
For two vertices a, b of G with characteristic vectors ea , eb , let their fidelity function be fa,b (t) =
|⟨eb , U (t)ea ⟩|. By Born’s rule, the probability of measuring b at time t given that the quantum walk
starts at a is fa,b (t)2 . We say G has pretty good state transfer (PGST) from a to b if for any ϵ > 0
there exists a time t0 so that fa,b (t0 ) ≥ 1 − ϵ (see [18, 36]); it is called perfect state transfer (PST)
if ϵ = 0 is realizable. Quantum state transfer is fundamental to quantum communication as it can
be used as a building block for protocols such as entanglement generation and state preparation
(see [11, 12, 1, 21, 23]).
Although PST/PGST are useful abstractions, there are several important obstacles in developing state transfer in practice. Arguably, PST is impossible to realize in real-world applications.
Recently, due to experimental challenges in realistic scenarios, a stronger emphasis has been placed
on high-fidelity state transfer (HFST) (see [8, 29, 30, 37, 14]). Here, for a small but fixed ϵ0 > 0,
we ask for an efficient time t0 for which fa,b (t0 ) ≥ 1 − ϵ0 . This differs markedly from PGST as ϵ0 is
not required to be arbitrarily close to 0 and time t0 must be a polynomial function in the relevant
parameters (such as graph size or diameter or transfer distance). Here, the emphasis is on transfer
time which is neither nondeterministic nor astronomical.
We explore a paradoxical idea (called T.rex ) which attaches weakly-coupled pendant edges (or
tiny arms) to a base graph (large body). This idea was studied by Wójcik et al. (see [38, 39]). Here,
we describe a mathematical treatment of this idea using the Feshbach-Schur method in perturbation
theory (see [22, 19]). Within this framework, we rigorously prove that high-fidelity quantum state
transfer occurs. The advantage of using the Feshbach-Schur method is that it is elementary and it
insulates asymptotic arguments (the bane of perturbation theory). We also prove that the transfer
time of the T.rex scheme does not depend on the diameter. In contrast, methods based on strong
potentials exhibit exponential scaling with the diameter (see [29, 30]).
Another formidable obstacle for state transfer is Anderson localization (see [4, 26, 33]). By
definition, localization is the absence of transport across a spin chain due to random diagonal
perturbations. We show how the T.rex idea overcomes this obstacle by constructing a robust state
transfer protocol which has high fidelity. Our protocol exploits nonsingularities of the underlying
spin chains in a direct way along with the use of a few clean qubits.

The following notion of high-fidelity state transfer is relevant to our results.
Definition 1. (High-fidelity state transfer)
Let G be a graph with adjacency matrix A(G). Let a, b be two vertices of G. We say G has highfidelity state transfer from a to b if for any ϵ ∈ (0, 1) there is an effectively computable time t = t(ϵ)
so that
| ⟨b| e−iA(G)t |a⟩ | ≥ 1 − ϵ.
(25)
In most cases, t(ϵ) is a polynomial function in 1/ϵ which may also depend on some properties of
the underlying graph G (through A(G), say). We will adopt the convenient Dirac notation which
is standard in quantum information. Thus, |α⟩ denotes a normalized column vector, while the row
vector ⟨α| = |α⟩† denote its conjugate transpose. The dimensions will be clear from context. For
δ > 0 and x ∈ R, let Bδ (x) denote the interval {y : |y − x| < δ}.

Let G be a graph with a nonsingular adjacency matrix A. Two normalized vectors α, β ∈ CV (G) are

| ⟨α| R(λ, A) |α⟩ − ⟨β| R(λ, A) |β⟩ | = γ0 | ⟨α| R(λ, A) |β⟩ |,

for all λ ∈ Bη (0)

For a graph G, we use R(λ, G) in place of R(λ, A). Recall that we say two vertices a, b in G are

5.3. Quantum Search on Edge. In quantum spatial search (see [16, 10]), given a graph G =
(V, E) with adjacency matrix A, we ask if there is γ (global scaling) so that for any vertex α ∈ V
(oracle location), we have τ (search time) such that
| ⟨α| e−iτ (γA+|α⟩⟨α|) |ρ⟩ | ≥ 1 − ϵ
where |ρ⟩ is the normalized Perron eigenvector of G. This captures an analog variant of Grover
search on arbitrary graphs. The quadratic speedup requirement is expressed as τ = O(1/| ⟨α|ρ⟩ |).
Typically, we require ϵ = o(1) but it can be relaxed to a bounded constant. In this framework,
spatial search can be viewed as reverse mixing (as we transform |ρ⟩ to |α⟩) under the rank-one
perturbation of A (induced by the oracle position).
Imagine a new framework where the oracle, instead of being a strong potential located at a
vertex, is now a pendant edge weakly coupled to G. To find this pendant edge (or oracle), we
randomly place another pendant edge on the graph (which, in the vertex model, is the Random
Target Lemma [28]). Next, we appeal to the T.rex argument to claim a high-fidelity state transfer
between the two pendant edges. For families of vertex-transitive graphs, we may use the hitting
times given in Table 1. In contrast, the same idea but with strong vertex potentials will run into
the same issues as outlined in Section 4.1.
6. Conclusions
The T.rex idea is a paradoxical, yet powerful, method for solving quantum transport problems.
We gave a mathematical treatment of the T.rex idea using the Feshbach-Schur method from perturbation theory [19]. A summary of our results and related open questions is given in the following:
(1) An elementary and rigorous proof that the T.rex method yields high-fidelity state transfer.
The requirements on the base graph is modest: it should have good condition number and
its two vertices involved in the state transfer must be γ-cospectral. In contrast to perfect
and pretty-good state transfer (see [13]), the latter condition is less stringent than strong
cospectrality. Also, see [14] for a related work on peak state transfer.
(2) The transfer time of the T.rex protocol does not depend directly on the diameter.
In contrast, the transfer time of protocols based on strong potentials increase exponentially
with the diameter (see [29, 30]). Is there a speed limit for high-fidelity state transfer?
(3) A high-fidelity state transfer protocol robust against localization in spin chains.
The key idea was to exploit nonsingularity of the underlying spin chains along with a few
protected vertices. Our protocol was able to overcome arbitrary noise (while possibly trading
off state transfer time), in sharp contrast to the negative results in [26].
(4) A simple method to find speedups in hitting times.
The versatility of the T.rex method was evident from the variety of graphs that were analyzed (thereby avoiding ad hoc methods). We then derived a quantum search algorithm to
locate an oracle hiding as a pendant edge.
To complement the above list, we mention other related results inspired by the T.rex paradigm:
(5) A simpler matrix inversion quantum algorithm [25].
This is based on attaching multiple T.rex arms to a nonsingular base graph.
(6) An optimal PST protocol robust against timing sensitivities on spin chains [24].
This exploits a link between weak couplings and the second derivative of the PST curve.
We strongly suspect there are further uses of the T.rex method waiting to be unearthed.
